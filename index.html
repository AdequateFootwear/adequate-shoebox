Gordy hopes to end up in a cache in Bobcaygeon, Ontario, a small town made famous by a song. Wawa, Ontario, home of the giant Wawa Goose, has a song too: "Little Wawa," by Stompin' Tom Connors. Find a cache in a place that's named in a song: a town, city, neighborhood, street, river, lake, mountain, or landmark, in any genre or language (whole states, provinces, and countries are too easy!). About

Gordy Goose (Gordon, formally) is one of the True North Trackers from Landsharkz, a Canadian geocaching shop: four trackable characters billed as "Canada's newest voyagers," alongside Beaver Dude, Otter Gal, and Cougar Annie. Gordy is a Canada goose dressed for dinner in a green jacket, red bow tie, and CANADA name badge, studying a trail map marked with X's. His dream is to end up in a cache in Bobcaygeon, Ontario. If you know, you know: "Bobcaygeon" is a 1999 single by the Tragically Hip that won the Juno Award for Single of the Year. Gord Downie said any small town would have suited the song; he picked Bobcaygeon partly because it was the only place name he could find that nearly rhymed with "constellation." The band finally played a concert there in 2011. The real Bobcaygeon, the "Hub of the Kawarthas," is built on three islands and the mainland, and it's home to the first lock built on the Trent–Severn Waterway, in 1833. The True North Trackers were the centerpiece of Adequate's event "Oie ? Oie ! Je suis une oie." (GCBWPED), held at the Wawa Goose on Highway 17 on September 5, 2026. Wawa has a song of its own: Stompin' Tom Connors's "Little Wawa" tells the legend of a goose who stayed behind when her flock flew south and gave the town her name. The name itself, according to the heritage sign beside the Goose, comes from clear water: Wawa Creek was called wawank, Ojibwe for "clear water springs," and it flowed out of a lake called wawagonk, "place of clear water," which settlers shortened to Wawa. Somewhere along the way, the name may have been mistaken for we'we, "snow goose," and the wild goose legend stuck. A giant goose has greeted travelers there since 1960, and the current bronze one was unveiled on Canada Day 2017, for Canada 150.

Your mission

Iinang is the Haida word for herring, and it also means "plentiful." On the Northwest Coast, plenty is tied to respect: many peoples, including the Haida, follow ancestral rules for treating salmon well, like returning salmon bones to the water, so that the salmon keep coming back. Two of the Haida guiding principles are Isda ad dii gii isda, "giving and receiving," and Yahguudang, "respect." People work much the same way: they come back when they're treated with respect. In a few sentences, describe one thing you do consistently to show respect to others in the geocaching community and help keep it going, and tell us about a time you did it. Those adjacent to the community count too: landowners, park staff, neighbors near GZ, or the curious non-cacher wondering what you're doing! No AI for this one.

Its goal

To stay in Adequate's collection. This salmon has already swum home!

About

Native Salmon Geocoin, a 3D geocoin from Geoswag in antique gold, shaped like a salmon with the hooked jaw a male grows on his way upstream to spawn. Its design is inspired by northern Northwest Coast formline art, the tradition of the Haida, Tlingit, Tsimshian, Nisga'a, Gitxsan, Haisla, and Heiltsuk peoples. Across the Northwest Coast, salmon are honored as relatives who return each year to feed the people, a return that depends on respect: in Haida practice, for one, salmon bones are given back to the river so that the salmon will come back.

The other side is built for leaf peeping: a stone farmhouse among red and orange trees under a big autumn sky. In the Latvian folk calendar, the autumn equinox brings Miķeļi, which absorbed the older harvest festival Apjumības. Families mowed the last field but left a small bundle of grain standing for Jumis, the spirit of the harvest, and dug beneath the last sheaf to look for him among the insects and worms there. Stalks with two ears grown together, called jumīši, were woven into a crown and a belt, and the Jumis sign, two crossed ears of grain, went up on rooftops for prosperity, a bit like a barn quilt on a barn. The Latvia block of Canada's Quilt of Belonging features the jumis too. Barn quilts bring quilt blocks outdoors. In 2001, Donna Sue Groves organized the first official quilt trail in Adams County, Ohio, to honor her mother, Maxine, a noted quilter; the first barn quilt was an Ohio Star. Quilt trails now wind through dozens of states and several Canadian provinces, including Ontario.
